\chapter{Function verification}
To verify the functionality of the device, a serie of basic measurements was performed. Later on, the injection pulse generator was tested and first synthetized pulses were received. Finally, a real \emph{SiPM} performance was measured.

\section{Measurements}
Multiple measurements were done to verify the basic functionality of the device. A \SI{200}{\mega\hertz} DSO-X 2024A oscilloscobe with a \SI{350}{\mega\hertz} probe has been used to carry out the measurements. Measurements on the power supplies were done as close to the supply output as possible. A spring contact was used to contact the ground of the probe to minimize the loop inductance of the probe.
\subsection{Power supplies}
The output ripple of the step down DC/DC converter was measured to be \SI{26}{\milli\volt} peak-to-peak. The frequency of the ripple measured coresponds well to the switching frequency of the converter (\SI{750}{\kilo\hertz}) \cite{diodes_ap62201} which can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:dcdc_ripple}. Note that the cursor frequency is measured over the span of 5 periods to get better accuracy, thus the measured value has to be multiplied by 5. For the LDOs, there was no observable ripple on the output. Both of these confirm that the power network integrity of the device is very good.

From Figure \ref{fig:hv_ripple}, it can be seen that a ripple of \SI{45}{\milli\volt} peak-to-peak was observed, which is low enough to avoid causing any issues with the biased sensors. These sensors will experience an even cleaner supply voltage due to the low-pass filtering. Figure \ref{fig:hv_transient} shows the transient response of the power supply. When the load is connected, the output voltage drops by \SI{100}{\milli\volt} and takes \SI{150}{\micro\second} to stabilize back to the setpoint. This will not cause any problems, as this voltage drop is small enough to avoid triggering any other sensors. The typical pulse from the connected sensor will also be much shorter, on the order of a few hundred nanoseconds, causing a much smaller voltage drop. Thus, the evaluated case represents a worst-case scenario.

\section{Injection pulse test}
For the injection pulse test, the FastIC+ 1 was configured to enable the injection signal routing into channel 5. The injection pulse generator was than enabled to generate the injection signal ($\sim$\SI{100}{\nano\second} pulses with $\sim$\SI{100}{\micro\second} period). Streaming of the aurora data to the USB was than started and \SI{100}{\milli\second} capture of the bitsream was created. The python library was than used to parse the raw data into packets. The packets for a first pulse detection are presented bellow.
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
[DATA] 
   Channel: 8 
   Packet type: PKT_TOA_TOTNL 
   Timestamp absolute (ps): 43951762448.88306 
   Pulse width (ps): 1025781.25 

[DATA] 
   Channel: 5 
   Packet type: PKT_TOA_TOTLN 
   Timestamp absolute (ps): 43953254158.78296 
   Pulse width (ps): 91796.875 

\end{verbatim}
\end{small}
\newpage
Along with a second detection that is subsequent to the fist one.
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
[DATA] 
   Channel: 8 
   Packet type: PKT_TOA_TOTNL 
   Timestamp absolute (ps): 44054708425.14038 
   Pulse width (ps): 1025000.0 

[DATA] 
   Channel: 5 
   Packet type: PKT_TOA_TOTLN 
   Timestamp absolute (ps): 44056162624.7406 
   Pulse width (ps): 92187.5 
  
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

The packets received on channel 5 correspond to the \emph{ToA} and \emph{ToT} of the pulse, transferred in the \verb|PKT_TOA_TOTLN| packet. The packet on channel 8 is the \emph{ToA} and \emph{ToT} of the trigger pulse that starts the \emph{FSM}. These differ due to different thresholds and processing on the separate paths. Taking the \emph{ToA} information from the channel 5 packets, it can be seen that a packet with a \emph{ToT} of \SI{91.797}{\nano\second} was detected first, followed by the second detection with \emph{ToT} of \SI{92.188}{\nano\second}. The \emph{ToT} corresponds nicely to the set generator pulse width. Taking the difference between the \emph{ToA} timestamps of the truggers, the period of the pulses can be calculated as $T = \SI{44054708425.14038}{\pico\second} - \SI{43951762448.88306}{\pico\second} = \SI{102.946e6}{\pico\second}$, which corresponds to the set period of \SI{100}{\micro\second} of the injection generator. The inaccuracy of both the \emph{ToA} and \emph{ToT} most likely originates from the injection generator rather than the FastIC+, which is very precise. The purpose of the injection is to easily check that the FastIC+ can receive pulses and process them correctly, rather than verifying the FastIC+'s accuracy. 

\section{SiPM threshold scan}
A common test performed on a physical \emph{SiPM} is a threshold scan. In this test, the \emph{SiPM} is connected to the readout electronics and placed in a completely dark environment. The threshold of the detection comparator is then varied across its full range, and the number of detected photopulses is recorded for each threshold setting. This experiment typically yields a staircase-like curve, where each step represents the number of dark counts at a given photon threshold level. \cite{onsemiAND9770}


Dark counts in the sensor are primarily caused by thermally generated electrons within the material. Based on the resulting plot, a suitable threshold is selected for actual measurements with the \emph{SiPM} to minimize the influence of dark counts \cite{onsemiAND9770}.

This experiment was conducted using a Hamamatsu \emph{SiPM}, model S13360-3025PE. The \emph{SiPM} was connected to the FastIC+ and placed in a dark box with multiple shielding layers to prevent any light leakage. To further minimize the risk of light intrusion, the experiment was performed at night in a completely dark room. The \emph{SiPM} was biased at \SI{56}{\volt}. The FastIC+ allows threshold adjustment over 64 discrete levels, and the full range was utilized. Under these conditions, the following plot was obtained:

The three lowest threshold values were excluded from the plot. At these settings, the channel primarily detects noise, resulting in saturation and a high number of error packets on the \emph{Aurora} link. This behavior is unexpected, and at the time of writing, I am collaborating with the chip designers to investigate the root cause.

The remaining data is very clean, and the expected staircase pattern is clearly visible. The first step corresponds to the threshold level required to detect single-photon events, the second step to two-photon events, and so on. Beyond threshold level 40, the number of detected hits becomes very low, and due to the limited data sample size, the staircase structure becomes indistinguishable from noise. For practical measurements, a threshold above level 40 is typically chosen to minimize the effect of dark counts.

Another notable observation is that the first step level (approximately \SI{800}{\kilo\count\per\second}) closely matches the datasheet’s typical value of \SI{500}{\kilo\count\per\second} (with a maximum of \SI{1500}{\kilo\count\per\second}). Variations in dark count rate can be attributed to temperature and bias voltage differences, which explains the discrepancy between the measured and datasheet values \cite{hamamatsuS13360}.

This exact experiment has been also repeated at CERN in a professional dark environment and resulted in the same trend for the dark counts.
